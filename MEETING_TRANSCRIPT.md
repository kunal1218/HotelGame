## 01. Original Apartment Exploration Concept

The discussion began with the idea that successful games often have a strong element of randomness.

Players should not know exactly what they will discover or what their next progression opportunity will be.

The original setting was an apartment building with many rooms.

Rather than filling the building with repetitive, ordinary apartments, rooms could contain strange objects, challenges, surprises, valuable resources, or completely unexpected situations.

Some rooms would be ordinary, making the unusual rooms more surprising.

Players could collect objects and explore while avoiding villains or hunters that appear in different locations.

The chasing mechanic was compared to *Scooby-Doo*, where characters run between doors, hallways, and staircases while trying to avoid whoever is pursuing them.

The comparison to *Hello Neighbor* introduced the idea of navigating a building while being hunted.

Players could place surveillance cameras in corridors to observe hunters and choose different escape routes.

For example, a player could notice a hunter moving through one hallway and escape using a stairwell instead.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Randomized room contents
- Collectibles and useful items
- Roaming hunters
- Multiple escape routes
- Hallways and stairwells
- Player-placed cameras
- Unpredictable chase sequences
- Fun emerging from exploration

## 02. Gameplay Without a Heavy Story

The group discussed whether the game needed an overarching narrative.

One perspective was that a detailed story might develop later, but it was not necessary for the initial experience.

*Fall Guys* was mentioned as an example of a game where interesting gameplay situations can be more important than the narrative.

The goal was to create situations where players do things, unexpected events occur, and the results are entertaining.

The group questioned why the hunters would chase players.

One proposed solution was a task-based system in which players perform activities that cause NPCs to become hostile.

A system similar to the wanted stars in *GTA* was suggested.

The comparison to *Among Us* introduced objectives scattered throughout the building.

Completing tasks could cause danger to escalate or force players to travel through risky areas.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Gameplay-first design
- Tasks distributed throughout the map
- Wanted or aggression system
- NPC hostility triggered by player behavior
- Escalating danger
- Minimal initial storytelling

## 03. Alternative Game Modes and Horror

The group brainstormed a tactical mode involving opposing teams, authorities, explosives, and hostage-related objectives, with *Counter-Strike* as an inspiration.

This was discussed as a possible separate game mode rather than necessarily the main concept.

The original mode was described as a more absurd situation involving disproportionately aggressive authorities chasing players over relatively minor violations.

The conversation then returned to whether the game was supposed to be horror.

The group agreed that the game did not need to be purely scary, but hunters should still produce frightening encounters.

When an NPC catches a player, there could be a short horror-style grab or capture animation instead of simply ending the game instantly.

The importance of **clip-worthy reactions** was introduced.

The group wanted situations where someone sees a threatening NPC, panics, screams, starts running, and creates a funny moment that other players would want to share.

*The Shining* was mentioned as inspiration for unsettling hotel environments, including strange children and unexplained encounters.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Optional tactical modes
- Horror-style capture animations
- Frightening hunter behavior
- Unexpected supernatural NPCs
- Environmental horror
- Comedy mixed with tension
- Gameplay intended to create shareable clips

## 04. Hidden Stories and Easter Eggs

The group suggested introducing optional mysteries inside the hotel.

One example was a cult operating somewhere in the building.

Players might discover clues, hidden rooms, strange symbols, or other evidence while exploring.

A possible starting premise involved checking into an ordinary apartment or hotel, going to sleep, and hearing unexplained knocking.

The group liked the idea of apparently ordinary situations gradually becoming supernatural.

The game was compared to *Lethal Company*, but with a hotel setting.

The group acknowledged that describing a game as another successful game in a different environment was not necessarily a problem.

The important question was whether the experience would be fun.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Optional cult storyline
- Discoverable clues
- Hidden areas
- Environmental storytelling
- Paranormal encounters
- Strange sounds and unexplained events
- Horror layered over the main gameplay

## 05. Why Players Need Objectives

The group identified a central gameplay problem:

Without objectives, players could simply hide in a closet and avoid danger.

Players need reasons to leave safety and enter uncomfortable situations.

An escape objective was proposed.

Players might begin in one room and attempt to find an exit, only to discover that doors lead into unexpected spaces.

This shifted the setting toward the *Backrooms*.

Instead of a conventional ten-story apartment building, the world could become a sprawling labyrinth containing many interconnected rooms.

It could resemble an abandoned hospital, apartment complex, or another surreal structure.

Five players would begin together and branch out to explore.

They would need to map the environment and complete objectives while monsters disrupted their plans.

The group emphasized difficult decisions.

For example, a player might have to choose between completing an important task and saving a friend who is being chased.

The discussion compared this to chess, where interesting gameplay emerges from calculating risks and choosing between imperfect options.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Shared five-player starting room
- Nonlinear labyrinth
- Exploration and mapping
- Escape as a possible objective
- Tasks that force movement
- Monsters that disrupt progress
- Time pressure
- Meaningful player decisions
- Risk assessment

## 06. Surreal Rooms and Money-Making Challenges

The group discussed the supernatural world in *Insidious* as inspiration for spaces where strange, unrelated events occur.

The rooms did not need to fit one consistent architectural theme.

A player might find a casino, an ordinary kitchen, a violent encounter, or a completely bizarre NPC.

The conversation introduced money as a possible progression mechanic.

Players could enter rooms and complete challenges to earn currency.

The casino example became particularly important.

A player might travel a long distance to reach a casino and take a major risk to earn enough money for an important weapon or survival upgrade.

Players would need to balance the reward against the threat of being hunted.

The group felt this could improve on a perceived weakness of *Lethal Company*: many rooms eventually become predictable or relatively uninteresting.

Rather than merely collecting items and returning them for money, rooms could contain unique challenges and opportunities.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Surreal and unrelated room themes
- Casino and gambling challenges
- Currency rewards
- Equipment purchases
- Risk-versus-reward decisions
- Dangerous optional activities
- Rooms with distinct gameplay mechanics
- Exploration as a major attraction

## 07. Persistent Progression and Movable Home Bases

The group discussed how different areas in *Lethal Company* feel disconnected, with progress frequently resetting.

An alternative was a continuous labyrinth that players explore progressively.

The starting room could initially serve as a home base.

As players discover more of the world, they could move their home point closer to rewarding or dangerous areas.

That would give the world a stronger feeling of permanent progress.

The group considered how players could expand farther into the labyrinth instead of repeatedly starting from the same place.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Continuous world progression
- Persistent explored territory
- Relocatable home base
- Increasingly rewarding areas
- More dangerous regions farther from safety
- Less reliance on disconnected levels

## 08. Visual Style and Deceptive Monsters

The group discussed how much of the game's visual design could be created using AI.

The team still wanted creative control over the hunters and overall atmosphere because those details would strongly affect the game's identity.

The conversation introduced monsters that deceive players.

One example was a monster generating fake footsteps to lure someone out of hiding.

Another was an NPC that repeats a teammate's previously spoken voice lines.

The group referenced voice-controlled games, including games where players cast abilities by speaking and multiplayer games where making particular sounds is part of the challenge.

The comparison to *Prop Hunt* introduced the broader idea of players or enemies pretending to be something else.

The group returned to *Lethal Company* and its exploration mechanics.

They liked the uncertainty of opening a door without knowing what would be inside, but wanted rooms that were more interactive and varied.

**Ideas introduced:**

- AI-assisted visual design
- Intentional monster design
- Fake footsteps
- Teammate voice mimicry
- Voice-related gameplay
- Deception and misinformation
- Unpredictable encounters
- Richer room activities

## 09. AI-Directed Gameplay

One participant reflected on the difficulty of designing a game that stays entertaining forever.

The group discussed using AI models to examine the current state of a match and introduce challenges dynamically.

Instead of relying exclusively on predetermined NPC behavior, the game could use AI to determine interesting situations.

For example, the system could examine player locations and choose a hunter spawn that creates a dramatic encounter.

The explicit goal was to generate memorable and highly shareable moments.

This became the concept of an AI-controlled game director.

**Ideas introduced:**

- AI monitoring the game state
- Dynamic challenges
- Contextual hunter appearances
- Unpredictable events
- Adaptive encounter pacing
- Clip-oriented gameplay
- Experiments with AI-generated situations

A beautiful initial room was proposed.

The environment could gradually become darker and more disturbing as players ventured farther.

A comparison was made to a movie involving catacombs and approaching hell.

**Ideas introduced:**

## 11. Progression Toward Satan

The discussion proposed a long-term progression ending in hell, potentially with players physically fighting Satan.

This created an exaggerated final destination for the game.

The group returned to the unresolved question of what players would actually do to progress.

Although the visual direction and world concept were exciting, the gameplay objectives were still unclear.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Increasingly infernal environments
- Hell as a final region
- Satan as a possible final boss

## 12. AI-Controlled Betrayal and Moral Dilemmas

The group returned to AI-directed challenges.

A possible theme emerged: **money corrupts people**.

The AI could deliberately create opportunities for players to betray one another.

For example, a player might be offered a financial reward for sabotaging or abandoning a teammate.

These situations could produce dramatic reactions and funny clips.

The group explicitly connected shareable moments to marketing.

The AI could also influence the placement of rooms or connections in the map according to the current situation.

The team discussed deciding progression rules before generating the world, because the progression system would determine room locations and distances.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Secret betrayal opportunities
- Money as a moral temptation
- Player-versus-player sabotage
- AI-generated dilemmas
- Dynamic room placement
- Social conflict as entertainment
- Progression-aware map generation

## 13. Tutorial Design and the Endless Hotel

The group compared the first room to early levels in *Mario*, where the player learns mechanics through a simple, intuitive environment.

The first task should teach rules that the game can later manipulate.

An example was an NPC asking players to collect objects or complete a morally questionable quest in exchange for money.

The conversation questioned whether the inhabitants of the strange hotel understood where they were.

One idea was that the residents had become accustomed to their environment and did not realize they were trapped.

The Lotus Casino from *Percy Jackson* was mentioned.

The hotel could be an endless luxury environment where guests indulge themselves indefinitely.

Once someone enters, they cannot escape without progressing through the world and defeating Satan.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Tutorial through simple tasks
- NPC quest-givers
- Residents unaware of their captivity
- Endless luxury hotel
- Trapped population
- Escape through progression
- Satan as the final obstacle

## 14. Seven Deadly Sins

The group suggested using the Seven Deadly Sins as inspiration for the game's regions.

One example was Gluttony, represented by an endless fast-food restaurant where nobody wants to leave.

Greed could involve a casino or an environment encouraging players to accumulate money.

The luxurious hotel itself could represent comfort and temptation.

The group liked the idea of players gradually progressing through increasingly corrupt environments.

Hunters could be agents of Satan sent to stop players attempting to escape.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Seven Deadly Sins
- Sin-inspired rooms
- Luxury and comfort as temptations
- NPCs trapped in indulgence

## 15. Competition Instead of Mandatory Cooperation

The group reconsidered whether everyone needed to be on the same team.

A competitive race was proposed.

Players could enter different doors, complete different objectives, and attempt to reach the end before their friends.

They could form temporary alliances, make agreements, and later betray those agreements.

The group considered giving players private HUD objectives and different personal motivations.

A death or capture would not permanently eliminate a player.

Instead, the player might respawn several rooms behind, creating a setback without making victory impossible.

The discussion compared this progression to *Fall Guys* and competitive games with respawn delays.

To prevent players from simply sprinting to the end, each region could contain a locked gate requiring objectives to be completed.

The requirements could encourage conflict, negotiation, and cooperation.

Dante's *Inferno* was mentioned as an inspiration.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Competitive race
- One eventual winner
- Temporary alliances
- Betrayal
- Private objectives
- Different player motivations
- Respawning farther behind
- Progression gates
- Conditions that encourage interaction
- Seven regions leading toward hell

## 16. Karma and Social Strategy

The group acknowledged that the competitive structure might move the game slightly away from traditional horror.

The gameplay could primarily create urgency, with scary NPCs and supernatural events supporting the experience.

A karma mechanic was proposed.

Players who behave selfishly or immorally could lose karma.

The AI director might be more likely to introduce hostile encounters for players with low karma.

Conversely, players with high karma might avoid certain punishments but miss out on profitable opportunities.

The group discussed earning positive karma by helping friends.

The goal was to create a social environment where people work together while remaining competitors.

*Survivor* and *Love Island* were suggested as references for understanding alliances, emotional conflict, and social dynamics.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Karma score
- Good and bad actions
- High-risk selfish rewards
- Safer cooperative strategies
- AI behavior influenced by karma
- Social manipulation
- Reality-TV-inspired player dynamics

## 17. Choosing the Seven Environments

The group questioned whether each region should rigidly represent one sin.

A visually memorable first region was considered important because many players might never reach later stages.

The first environment should create entertaining clips and hook players immediately.

An adult nightlife setting was proposed as one possibility.

However, the group recognized that restricting every room to one sin could reduce variety.

Instead, each region could have a recognizable central environment, while the surrounding rooms could contain unrelated activities.

Greed could involve entering an enormous urban city where players can join factions, earn money, or betray others.

Other examples included war zones, poor cities, casinos, and lawless environments.

The group began favoring **environments that encourage morally difficult decisions** rather than environments that simply symbolize particular sins.

For example, a city without police could allow players to steal, rob, or exploit people.

However, helping someone might produce a better reward later.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Distinct central themes
- Mixed room environments
- Strong visual hook
- Nightlife district
- Lawless urban city
- War environments
- Morally exploitable social rules
- Rewards for both selfish and helpful choices

## 18. Starting Together and Branching Out

The group considered whether all players should begin each level together.

The preference was to have everyone see one another at the beginning.

This could encourage social interaction and alliances.

However, players would need different objectives or incentives to branch out instead of all following the same route.

The group recognized that designing seven fully independent worlds would be too large an initial undertaking.

They decided it would be better to build one small environment, test the central mechanic, and expand only if it was fun.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Shared starting location
- Different personal objectives
- Branching exploration
- Small initial prototype
- Expand only after validation

## 19. Prototype-First Development

The group discussed how developers sometimes release an initial experience with larger content locked or unfinished, then expand after the game gains traction.

More importantly, they wanted to avoid becoming committed to a massive project before validating whether the gameplay was actually entertaining.

The suggested approach was to begin with the first hotel room.

Questions included:

- What would players expect to find behind the first door?
- What surprising environment could appear instead?
- How could the game immediately teach its central mechanics?
- What experience would make players want to continue?

The group agreed that the game could begin with everyone checking into the same hotel.

A short introductory story could explain how they arrived.

## 20. Different Sinners and Character Classes

The group discussed having different types of people checking into the hotel.

They could come from different backgrounds and have different personalities.

The concept of character classes was introduced.

A businessman was suggested as a profit-oriented class.

The group considered other archetypes with distinct motivations and abilities.

Social deduction games with detectives and killers were referenced as inspiration for players having different roles.

The karma system was seen as a natural way to discourage indiscriminate attacks.

Early in the game, violent behavior could carry a major karma penalty.

Farther toward hell, the environment might increasingly encourage or tolerate immoral actions.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Character classes
- Different starting abilities
- Cosmetics
- Combat roles
- Class-based incentives
- Karma penalties for violence
- Moral standards changing by region

## 21. Four Character Archetypes

The group moved toward four starting archetypes.

**Businessman**

A profit-focused character who maximizes financial returns, negotiates, and manages resources.

**Teacher**

A humanitarian or knowledgeable character who can help people and use knowledge creatively.

**Police Officer / Authority Figure**

A character with authority who could protect people or abuse that authority.

**Socially Persuasive Character**

A charismatic character who can influence NPCs and other people, gain favors, and change alliances.

The group discussed contrasting personalities in an ensemble cast.

*Squid Game*, Indian ensemble comedies, and drama series were referenced.

The idea was to put very different characters into the same situation and observe how they interact.

## 22. Individual Tutorials and a Shared Incident

The group proposed beginning with a normal hotel lobby.

Players would check in together, receive rooms, and explore the initial environment.

Each character might have a short tutorial connected to their class.

For example, the businessman could take a business call.

The other characters might experience small interactions reflecting their personalities or abilities.

After the first night or some other triggering event, all players would be forced out of their rooms.

A fire alarm was suggested.

The group felt this would create a natural reason for the characters to meet and enter a shared storyline.

However, they later questioned whether individualized storylines would be too much work.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Normal hotel opening
- Individual room assignments
- Class-based tutorial interactions
- First night
- Fire alarm or similar incident
- Shared hallway
- Unexpected event that begins the game

## 23. Simplifying Character Design

The group decided that elaborate, character-specific stories might be unnecessary.

Characters could simply have gameplay strengths and weaknesses based on their class.

Quests could remain generic, randomized, and reusable across different character types.

The game could introduce almost any unusual situation through an environmental transition.

One example was a nuclear explosion destroying the surrounding world while leaving the hotel intact.

Players would emerge from the hotel into a chaotic wasteland.

The group compared the potential atmosphere to *Fallout* and *Mad Max*.

The main idea was to place players in environments with limited rules and difficult survival decisions.

## 24. Wasteland Survival and Class Abilities

In the wasteland concept, players could have survival requirements such as food, sleep, and hydration.

A water source might be controlled by hostile NPCs.

Players could fight to obtain access, negotiate, or find a different solution.

Actions would influence karma.

The group refined the four class abilities:

- **Businessman:** Negotiation, trading, financial management, efficient resource use
- **Teacher:** Knowledge, crafting, understanding materials, fixing things
- **Police Officer:** Combat, weapons, protection, handling threats
- **Persuasive Character:** Influence, persuasion, NPC relationships, convincing others to help

The group questioned whether cooperation should be required.

One suggestion was that it could depend on the level or situation.

Some challenges might require cooperation while others encourage direct competition.

## 25. Hotel as the Last Safe Base

The group developed the idea that the hotel could survive a catastrophe while the outside world became dangerous.

The hotel would remain the main base.

Outside, players could encounter wasteland environments, hostile groups, mutations, and potentially different dimensions.

The world could become progressively more supernatural.

To keep the hotel functioning, players might need to collect resources.

Electricity and other essential systems would require maintenance.

If the hotel lost power or shelter, the players would become vulnerable.

This finally created a concrete reason to leave the hotel.

**Ideas introduced:**

- Hotel as central shelter
- Resource expeditions
- Generator maintenance
- Food and water needs
- Hostile NPC-controlled resources
- Mutated monsters
- Different dimensions
- Threat of losing shelter
- Shared survival responsibilities

## 26. Final Development Discussion

The group recognized that sitting inside the hotel without objectives would not provide much gameplay.

They considered the hotel survival concept a potential starting point.

Someone mentioned the impressive early demo and requested that the repository be shared.

The team discussed working on separate Git branches and maintaining a shared backlog for ideas.

They also recognized that broader game-design changes could conflict if everyone implemented separate features simultaneously.

The final development philosophy was to prototype rapidly.

The group felt AI-assisted development made it possible to test several different game concepts without committing to one immediately.

The key principle was:

**If a prototype is not fun to play, do not keep expanding it.**

The group wanted to discover which mechanics were entertaining before investing heavily in worldbuilding, assets, or large-scale implementation.

## 27. Current State at the End of the Meeting

The discussion did not establish one fully approved game design.

The strongest recurring concepts were:

1. A multiplayer experience with roughly five players
2. A mysterious hotel as the original setting or central hub
3. Randomized exploration and surprising environments
4. Objectives that require players to leave safety
5. Distinct character abilities
6. Money, resources, and risk-reward decisions
7. Cooperation, competition, and possible betrayal
8. Karma and consequences for player behavior
9. Hunters and horror-style encounters
10. AI-directed dynamic challenges
11. Progression into increasingly dangerous areas
12. Rapid prototyping before expanding the game

Two larger directions remained open:

**Direction A — Competitive Descent:** Players compete through surreal regions associated with sin, completing challenges and progressing toward hell.

**Direction B — Hotel Survival:** Players maintain a central hotel while venturing into dangerous environments to gather resources.

The group also considered hybrid approaches.

The immediate next step was to share the repository, maintain a development backlog, implement small testable mechanics, and playtest together.
